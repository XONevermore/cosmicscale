A neutron star skims past Earth

A dead star the size of a city overtakes Earth: its beam, its tide, and how it takes the Moon and then Earth away from the Sun

A what-if: physics allows it on paper, but no one has ever seen one. Everything shown follows the theory as it stands.

Watch it in 3D

Step by step

How one is made, and sent flying

To make one, you need a star of at least eight Suns. When its core runs out of fuel it collapses in less than a second, crushing protons and electrons together into neutrons. The rest of the star is blown away in a supernova, and the core stays behind.

The blast is never perfectly even, so the core recoils like a gun. Young neutron stars typically travel at hundreds of km/s, and a few exceed 1,000 km/s (Hobbs et al., 2005). Our galaxy may hold up to a billion of them, almost all too faint to see. No one has seen one pass through a star's planets: this visit is imagined.

Through the planets in months

The danger is being right next to the path. Here the star comes from behind and overtakes Earth just outside its orbit, moving the same way. The Sun is dragged a little too, though it is far enough away to keep its planets. Earth is the one world in the star's way.

The closest day

The gamma rays are the worst of it. None reach the ground directly, because the air stops them, but the air takes all that energy for hours. The top of the atmosphere on the side facing the star would be heated and torn apart. No one has modelled this in detail, and it depends on the beam really pointing at Earth.

The Moon breaks free

Earth and the Moon go on travelling the same way, slowly drifting apart, and within weeks the Moon is a bright star in the sky and no longer a disc. Earth keeps only the Sun's small tides. There are no more eclipses and no more moonlit nights.

Thrown out of the Solar System

Earth moves at 30 km/s and needs 42 km/s to escape the Sun. The star overtakes it from behind and pulls it forward, far past that speed. The Sun shrinks to a very bright star within a year. In all of this, the star barely changes course and carries on out of the Solar System.

How likely?

So this is a story about what such a star is, not a warning. Astronomers have surveyed the stars around us, and no known neutron star is on its way here. The closest one we know, RX J1856, is about 400 light-years off. A real Geminga-like star this close would have been seen long ago.
