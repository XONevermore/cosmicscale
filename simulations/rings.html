What if Earth had a ring?

A ring of rock round the equator, like Saturn's: how one could form, how it would look from the ground, and the shadow it would cast

A what-if: physics allows it on paper, but no one has ever seen one. Everything shown follows the theory as it stands.

Watch it in 3D

Step by step

How one could form

Earth pulls harder on the near side of a passing body than on the far side. For a loose pile of rock, inside the Roche limit that difference wins over the body's own gravity, and it comes apart. Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 did exactly this at Jupiter in 1992, breaking into more than 20 pieces that struck the planet two years later.

Most pieces from such a breakup would fly on or fall, but some would stay in orbit. Colliding again and again, they would lose their up-and-down motion and flatten into a thin disc. Earth's bulge at the equator turns tilted orbits slowly round, so the disc would end up in the equator's plane, as Saturn's rings are.

A ring, not a moon

Inside the Roche limit, Earth's tides keep tearing clumps apart, so the rock stays a ring. Outside it, the pieces would gather into a moon.

Within the ring, two boulders that drift together are pulled apart again: the tide across them is stronger than their pull on each other. That is why the rings of Saturn, Jupiter, Uranus and Neptune all lie close to their planets, inside their Roche limits, while their large moons orbit farther out.

Our own Moon is thought to have formed just outside Earth's Roche limit, from a disc of debris thrown up when a body the size of Mars struck the young Earth about 4.5 billion years ago. In computer models, the debris beyond the limit gathers into a moon in as little as a year, while the part inside falls back to Earth.

From the equator

Standing on the equator, you would see the ring edge on: a thin bright line crossing the sky from east to west, straight overhead.

The ring lies in the equator's plane, so from anywhere on the equator you look along it, edge on. Rings are very thin for their width; Saturn's main rings are mostly about ten metres thick across hundreds of thousands of kilometres. So the whole ring would shrink to a fine line, running from the eastern horizon through the point overhead to the western one.

The line would barely move through the day or the year: it is fixed to Earth's equator, not to the Sun. Twice a year, at the equinoxes, the Sun lies in the ring's plane and lights it only edge on, and the ring nearly disappears.

The winter shadow

Darker winters would mean colder winters. Tomkins and his colleagues suggested that their ancient ring's shadow may have helped start the Hirnantian ice age, one of the coldest spells of the last half-billion years, near the end of the Ordovician. How much colder a ring would make Earth today depends on its thickness and on how the air and oceans respond, which has not been modelled.

How long it would last

Not forever. Colliding boulders spread the ring out; those that reach the air at its inner edge fall, and the outer ones drift beyond the Roche limit.

Saturn's rings may be just as passing. The Cassini probe saw them raining material into the planet, and some estimates give them a few hundred million years left. A ring is a short chapter in a planet's life, which is one reason Earth, 4.5 billion years old, has none today.
