Eclipses in 2026 and 2027

Every solar and lunar eclipse of 2026 and 2027, worked out from the orbits of the Earth and the Moon. A total solar eclipse is only total inside a narrow path; a lunar eclipse can be seen from the whole night side of Earth.

Annular solar eclipse

6 February 2027, peak 15:59 UTC

Where: A path across Chile, Argentina and the Atlantic to West Africa; partial from South America and Africa.

The Moon is too far from Earth to cover the whole Sun, so a bright ring of the Sun is left around the dark Moon. Everywhere off that narrow path, the Moon covers only part of the Sun.

Penumbral lunar eclipse

20 February 2027, peak 23:12 UTC

The Moon passes only through Earth's pale outer shadow, so it dims a little and no bite is taken. It is easy to miss, even with the Moon high up.

Earlier

Annular solar eclipse

17 February 2026, peak 12:11 UTC

Where: Antarctica; a partial eclipse from the southern tips of South America and Africa.

The Moon is too far from Earth to cover the whole Sun, so a bright ring of the Sun is left around the dark Moon. Everywhere off that narrow path, the Moon covers only part of the Sun.

Total lunar eclipse

3 March 2026, peak 11:33 UTC

Where: East Asia, Australia, the Pacific and the Americas.

The Moon slides into Earth's shadow and, for 59 minutes, is fully inside its darkest part. It does not vanish: sunlight bent through Earth's air still reaches it, so it glows dull red, lit by every sunrise and sunset on Earth at once.

Partial lunar eclipse

28 August 2026, peak 04:12 UTC

Where: The Americas, Europe, Africa and the eastern Pacific.

Part of the Moon crosses into the darkest part of Earth's shadow and looks as if a bite has been taken out of it. The curved edge you see is Earth's own shadow.
