What if we built a space elevator?

A cable from the equator to 100,000 km up, held taut by Earth's spin: why it would stay up, what it would need to be made of, and how it could be built

A what-if: physics allows it on paper, but no one has ever seen one. Everything shown follows the theory as it stands.

Step by step

A cable to space

None has ever been built, and none can be yet. Everything depends on a material that no one has made long enough, as the next steps show.

Why it stays up

So the cable must reach far beyond that height, or end in a heavy counterweight, such as a captured asteroid or the station that built it. The outer part pulls up on the lower part, and the whole cable stays stretched over the same point on the equator. It does not rest on its anchor; the anchor only holds it in place.

It is not a tower: nothing is pushed from below. The whole cable is under tension, greatest at the geostationary height, where the pull from above meets the weight from below.

And no one has made nanotubes that strong more than a few centimetres long: bundles stronger than 80 gigapascals were made in 2018 (Bai and others), but Nicola Pugno has shown that the flaws in any long cable would make it at least 70% weaker than a perfect tube.

How one could be built

A spacecraft carries a spool to the geostationary height and lets the ribbon out downward and upward at once, keeping its balance there. Then climbers widen it, strip by strip.

It cannot be built up from the ground: a tower that tall would crush itself. Edwards's plan starts in orbit. Rockets lift a spool of thin ribbon to the geostationary height. It is let out in both directions at once, down towards Earth and up away from it, so that its centre of mass stays at that height and keeps pace with the turning ground.

The anchor would float on the equator in the eastern Pacific, where hurricanes and lightning are rare, on a platform that could steer the cable out of harm's way.

The dangers

Every satellite and piece of debris whose orbit dips below the cable's top would one day cross its path. The cable would have to dodge them, and survive storms and the Moon's tugs.

Lower down it faces wind, lightning and atomic oxygen, which eats away at materials in the thin upper air. The Moon's and Sun's pulls and every climber's push set the cable swaying, and those waves would have to be damped by moving the anchor. If it ever snapped, the part below the break would fall back, much of it burning up in the air, and the part above would be flung outward.

Smaller worlds first

The Moon turns only once a month, too slowly for a cable to hang from a stationary orbit above it. Instead, a cable could run from its near side through the balance point between Earth's pull and the Moon's, about 58,000 km above the Moon's centre. Jerome Pearson worked this out in the 1970s; fibres such as Zylon would be strong enough.
