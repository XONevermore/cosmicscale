How the Solar System formed

From a collapsing cloud to a spinning disc, dust, pebbles, planetesimals and planets, and the impact that made the Moon

Watch it in 3D

Step by step

A cloud and a shock

What is measured and what is drawn: the age comes from dating the oldest grains in meteorites. The cloud is drawn from noise, not from a map of the real one; its fall is sped up from about a hundred thousand years to seconds, and the shock wave is drawn as a pale sheet, though a real one would be invisible.

The frost line

Ice matters because there is a lot of it. Outside the line, solids could gather about four times more mass than inside, where only rock and metal stayed solid. That is a big reason the Solar System has small rocky planets near the Sun and giant planets farther out. Asteroids in the outer part of the belt still hold water, and those in the inner part are drier.

What is measured and what is drawn: the temperature at which water freezes at the disc's low pressure is measured in the laboratory, while the line's place comes from models of the disc. The ring is drawn where the colours change, and the warm and icy colours of the disc are drawn to show the difference; they would not be seen.

Dust sticks together

What is measured and what is drawn: sticking and bouncing have been measured in drop towers and on the International Space Station, and ALMA sees rings in real discs where pebbles gather. Here the grains are drawn far bigger, and spread far more thinly, than they are, so that one can see them, and their growth is sped up.

Pebbles to planetesimals

After that, gravity does the work. Bigger bodies pull harder, so they sweep up the small ones fastest and run away from the rest, growing into embryos the size of the Moon or Mars. What is drawn: the swarms and the bodies are drawn far bigger and closer together than they are, and their growth is sped up.

The giants grow first

What is measured and what is drawn: the planets' masses today are measured from the orbits of their moons and of spacecraft. How the giants grew is modelled. The planets are drawn far bigger than they are, the gap is drawn wider and smoother than models give, and the growth is sped up.

Rocky planets collide into shape

Without the gas, dozens of embryos the size of the Moon or Mars cross orbits and collide. Over tens of millions of years they merge into Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars.

What is measured and what is drawn: the ages come from isotopes in Earth's rocks and in meteorites from Mars. The collisions here follow no particular model run: which embryo meets which is drawn, though the outcome, four planets at their real distances, is right. The bodies are drawn hundreds of times bigger than they are, and their orbits are sped up.

The giants spread out

What is measured and what is drawn: today's orbits are measured; the giants' early moves are a model that explains where Neptune, the Kuiper belt and the giants' tilted orbits are. At the end the planets are lined up in a row for the picture and drawn far bigger than they are, but their distances from the Sun are to scale.
