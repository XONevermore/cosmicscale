T Coronae Borealis: the star about to appear

A star too faint to see that flares up about every 80 years: how a stolen layer of gas ignites, and where to look when it does

Watch it in 3D

Step by step

A star waiting to appear

A star that does this again and again is called a recurrent nova. Only about ten are known in our galaxy, and T CrB is the brightest of them at its peak. Its behaviour now looks like the years before 1946, so astronomers expect it soon. As this was written, in November 2026, it had not happened yet, and no one can name the date.

Stolen gas

The white dwarf's gravity pulls gas off the red giant. The gas streams across the gap and winds into a glowing disc around the white dwarf.

A layer of fuel

The gas settles on the white dwarf as a thin layer of hydrogen. Year after year the layer gets heavier, and its bottom gets more squeezed and hotter.

The flash

In the Sun, fusion is steady: if the core gets too hot it swells and cools down again. A white dwarf's matter is packed too tightly to swell, so the heat has nowhere to go. More heat means faster fusion, which means more heat. The same runaway powers a hydrogen bomb, on the scale of a star.

Blown off, not destroyed

The heat lifts the layer off the white dwarf. The shell of gas races outwards and thins as it grows, wrecking the disc on its way. It washes over the red giant, which is far too big to be harmed, and carries on into space.

Where to look

Draw a line from Arcturus to Vega. About a third of the way along lies the small arc of the Northern Crown. The new star will appear just below the arc's left side.

Old records suggest eruptions in 1217 and 1787 as well (Schaefer, 2023), so people may have been watching this star for 800 years. It will do this again and again for as long as the red giant feeds it. One 2025 study of its orbit suggests the white dwarf is losing mass overall, so it will not end as a supernova.
